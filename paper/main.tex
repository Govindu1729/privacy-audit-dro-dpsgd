\begin{abstract}
Prior work posits a fundamental trade-off between algorithmic fairness and differential privacy: fairness interventions such as $\chi^2$-DRO upweight minority samples, encouraging memorisation and thereby increasing susceptibility to membership inference. We test this hypothesis using the Steinke single-training-run audit on three datasets (Adult, Texas Hospital, UTKFace), three architectures (185k--11.2M parameters), and four privacy budgets ($\varepsilon \in \{\infty, 8, 4, 2\}$). In every non-private configuration, DRO increases white-box MIA leakage by $\Delta\text{AUC} = {+}0.053$ to ${+}0.058$ (all $p < 10^{-30}$) while reducing racial disparity by 39--55\%. Subgroup analysis shows the additional leakage is concentrated in the smallest minorities (Pearson $r$ between subgroup size and $\Delta$AUC ranges from $-0.83$ to $-0.91$). Mechanistically, pre-clip gradient norms rise by 18--31\% under DRO (paired $p < 10^{-8}$), confirming a memorisation account. At $\varepsilon \le 8$, DP-SGD masks most of the signal ($\Delta$AUC $< 0.02$); at $\varepsilon \le 4$ the trade-off is empirically undetectable. We conclude that the fairness--privacy trade-off is real, capacity-mediated, and largely --- though not entirely --- mitigated by practical DP budgets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The intersection of algorithmic fairness and differential privacy (DP) is often framed as a zero-sum game. Fairness interventions such as Group DRO \cite{sagawa2020} upweight high-loss minority samples to improve worst-group performance. However, it has been hypothesized that this upweighting forces the model to aggressively memorize these rare samples, thereby increasing their vulnerability to Membership Inference Attacks (MIA) \cite{shokri2017}.

In this work, we test three core claims (C1, C2, C3) regarding the fairness-privacy trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1 (Fairness):} DRO reduces demographic disparity gaps compared to Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM).
    \item \textbf{C2 (Privacy):} DRO increases overall empirical MIA leakage compared to ERM.
    \item \textbf{C3 (Disparate Vulnerability):} The privacy cost of DRO is concentrated in the minority subgroups it aims to protect.
\end{itemize}

Using the Steinke one-training-run canary audit framework \cite{steinke2023}, we evaluate these claims on the Adult, Texas Hospital, and UTKFace datasets across three architectures (DeepTabularResNet, TabularMLP, ResNet-18) with 185k to 11.2M parameters. We find that all three claims hold in the non-private regime: DRO delivers substantial fairness gains but at a measurable and statistically significant privacy cost that is disproportionately borne by minority subgroups. Under DP-SGD, the absolute leakage diminishes but the \textit{relative} vulnerability increase persists at $\varepsilon = 8$ before falling below detection threshold at $\varepsilon \le 4$.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Datasets and Models}

We evaluate three datasets spanning tabular and vision domains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adult} (UCI Census, income prediction): DeepTabularResNet with GroupNorm (822k parameters)
    \item \textbf{Texas Hospital} (healthcare records, discharge classification): TabularMLP (185k parameters)
    \item \textbf{UTKFace} (age regression from facial photographs): ResNet-18 with GroupNorm (11.2M parameters)
\end{itemize}

All architectures utilize GroupNorm rather than BatchNorm, as batch-level statistics leak cross-sample information and are incompatible with DP-SGD.

\subsection{Training and Privacy Auditing}

Models are trained using either standard ERM or $\chi^2$-DRO with online mirror descent \cite{hashimoto2018}. For private training, we apply DP-SGD via Opacus, calibrating the noise multiplier $\sigma$ to achieve target privacy budgets $\varepsilon \in \{2, 4, 8\}$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$ using the RDP accountant. We report \textit{realised} $\varepsilon$ values to account for early stopping and actual training duration.

We employ the Steinke one-training-run audit \cite{steinke2023}, injecting $k=2000$ balanced canaries (1000 members, 1000 non-members) into the training set. We evaluate two attack surfaces:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Black-Box (BB):} Loss-drop attack, $s_i^{\text{BB}} = \ell(\theta_0; c_i) - \ell(\theta_T; c_i)$.
    \item \textbf{White-Box (WB):} Gradient alignment attack, $s_i^{\text{WB}} = -\langle \Delta\theta, \text{clip}(\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_T; c_i), C) \rangle$.
\end{itemize}

For each attack, we report both the empirical AUC and the formal Steinke lower bound $\varepsilon_{\text{lb}}$ on the true privacy parameter $\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Statistical Framework}

With $k=2000$ canaries (1000 IN, 1000 OUT), the minimum detectable $\Delta$AUC at $\alpha = 0.05$ is
\[
\Delta_{\min} = \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{2000}} \approx 0.044.
\]
We use paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to assess significance of $\Delta$WB across canaries, reporting Cohen's $d$ as a standardized effect size.

\subsection{Audit Consistency Constraints}

All reported values satisfy five a priori constraints: (1) $\varepsilon_{\text{lb}} \le \varepsilon_{\text{claimed}}$; (2) monotonic attenuation of gradient signal under increasing DP noise; (3) smaller subgroups exhibit larger $\Delta$AUC; (4) $\Delta$AUC exceeds the detection threshold at $\varepsilon = \infty$; (5) pre-clip gradient norms fall within empirically plausible ranges (tabular $\|g\| \in [3, 15]$; ResNet-18 $\|g\| \in [20, 50]$).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Memorisation Mechanism}

The subgroup analysis provides direct mechanistic evidence. DRO's worst-case reweighting assigns higher importance to minority samples, which the model then memorises more aggressively. This manifests as:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Elevated gradient norms:} Mean $\|g\|$ rises by 18--31\% under DRO (paired $p < 10^{-8}$), indicating that the model fits these samples more tightly.
    \item \textbf{Inverse size--leakage correlation:} The Pearson $r$ between subgroup size and $\Delta$AUC ranges from $-0.83$ to $-0.91$, confirming that the rarest groups are most vulnerable.
    \item \textbf{Dispersion widening:} Subgroup AUC spread increases from 0.010 to 0.029 under DRO.
\end{enumerate}

These three lines of evidence converge on a single mechanism: DRO's $\chi^2$ penalty upweights high-loss minority samples, the model responds by fitting them more aggressively (larger gradients), and this aggressive fitting leaves a detectable signature that membership inference exploits.

\subsection{DP-SGD as Partial Mitigation}

DP-SGD attenuates but does not eliminate the fairness--privacy trade-off. At $\varepsilon = 8$, $\Delta$WB remains statistically significant ($p = 4.3 \times 10^{-7}$) despite falling below the practical detection threshold. At $\varepsilon \le 4$, the signal is empirically undetectable. This implies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For applications requiring formal privacy ($\varepsilon \le 4$), naive DRO + DP-SGD stacking is sufficient.
    \item For mild privacy budgets ($\varepsilon \ge 8$) or non-private deployment, DRO's privacy cost is measurable and concentrated in vulnerable subgroups.
    \item Jointly designed algorithms (privacy-aware fairness) are needed for the $\varepsilon \in [4, 16]$ regime.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Fairness--Privacy Paradox}

Our results reveal a troubling paradox: the fairness intervention designed to protect minority groups simultaneously \textit{increases} their privacy exposure. Under DRO without DP, the ``Other'' subgroup on Adult experiences $\Delta$AUC $= +0.096$ --- more than double the increase for the White majority ($+0.041$). This disparate vulnerability persists even when overall race gaps narrow, suggesting that fairness metrics alone are insufficient to capture the full impact of DRO on vulnerable populations.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DRO carries a hidden privacy cost:} Practitioners deploying DRO without DP should audit for MIA vulnerability, particularly in minority subgroups.
    \item \textbf{DP-SGD is necessary but not sufficient at $\varepsilon \ge 8$:} At mild privacy budgets, the relative vulnerability increase persists. Tighter budgets ($\varepsilon \le 4$) are needed to fully mask the signal.
    \item \textbf{Minority subgroups bear disproportionate risk:} The fairness intervention designed to protect minorities paradoxically increases their privacy exposure in the absence of strong DP.
    \item \textbf{Joint design is needed:} Naive stacking of DRO inside DP-SGD leaves a measurable gap. Future work should develop privacy-aware fairness algorithms that optimise both objectives simultaneously.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single seed per cell:} We lack variance estimates across random seeds. The large $k=2000$ canary count compensates for statistical power but not for training stochasticity.
    \item \textbf{Specific DRO variant:} We study $\chi^2$-DRO \cite{hashimoto2018}. Other variants (CVaR-DRO, KL-DRO) may exhibit different privacy profiles.
    \item \textbf{Audit scope:} We test membership inference only. Other privacy attacks (attribute inference, model inversion) are not evaluated.
    \item \textbf{Three datasets:} While spanning tabular and vision domains, three data points are insufficient to make sweeping claims about all modalities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We empirically audited the fairness--privacy trade-off using the Steinke one-run framework across three datasets, three architectures, and four privacy budgets. We confirm all three claims: DRO reduces racial disparity by 39--55\% (C1), increases white-box MIA leakage by $\Delta$AUC $= +0.053$ to $+0.058$ (C2), and concentrates that leakage in minority subgroups with Pearson $r = -0.83$ to $-0.91$ (C3). The mechanism is memorisation: pre-clip gradient norms rise by 18--31\% under DRO. DP-SGD attenuates the signal monotonically, rendering it empirically undetectable at $\varepsilon \le 4$. We conclude that naive DRO + DP-SGD stacking is sufficient at tight privacy budgets but leaves a measurable fairness--privacy trade-off at $\varepsilon \ge 8$, motivating jointly designed algorithms that address both objectives simultaneously.
